Section 6 · Visual privacy and overlooking Source: GSA 2021-087, SEE p. 17–19

GSA 2021-087 · SEE · page 17
6.1 The relationship to No. 10 and No. 6

The balcony sits at first-floor level on the rear elevation. The rear private open space of No. 10 lies about 9 m to the south-east, and the living room windows of No. 6 about 11 m to the north-east. Without treatment, a person standing at the balustrade would have a direct, elevated view into both.

6.2 Design response

The balcony depth has been reduced from 2.4 m to 1.8 m, which keeps it ancillary to the main bedroom and avoids a large area of elevated outdoor space (§5.3 vi). It is not the primary private open space; that remains the ground-floor terrace and lawn. Fixed privacy screens 1.6 m above the finished floor level, 70% opaque (§5.3 iv), are proposed to the eastern and western edges. The first-floor windows are offset from those of the adjoining dwellings (§5.3 i). Boundary planting to 3–4 m is shown on landscape plan L-01 as a supplementary device only (§5.3 v).

6.3 Assessment

The sightline sections at Appendix C show that, with the screens in place, there is no direct sightline from the balcony into the rear private open space or living-room windows of either adjoining dwelling. The Court's privacy principle (Meriton v Sydney City Council [2004] NSWLEC 313) applies: at low density a dwelling and some of its open space can reasonably expect to stay private, and overlooking that comes from poor design is not acceptable where an alternative design gives the applicant the same amenity. The reduced depth and fixed screens are that alternative. Consistent with Super Studio v Waverley Council [2004] NSWLEC 91, no weight is placed on the proposed planting as the protection itself. The proposal meets the visual privacy objectives of Part C1 §5.3.
